Notes, sources & links

Where the facts come from, and what we made up.

What’s invented

Norland, Dana Reyes, Priya, Tom Hadley and the Bridge and Rail Act are all fictional. So are the game’s scoring rules: they decide how your choices play out in the story and are not measurements of how real governments behave.

What’s real

The “real case that looks like yours” in each debrief is an actual event. The cases currently used:

  • John Major, July 1993. A confidence vote tied to the Maastricht social chapter motion, won 339 to 299. Sources: UPI archive, Washington Post archive.
  • James Callaghan, 1978–79. Survived a confidence vote by ten in December 1978, then lost one 311 to 310 on 28 March 1979. Sources: ITV News history of confidence votes, Hansard, 28 March 1979, House of Commons Library, Confidence Motions.
  • Brazil’s provisional measures. Take effect immediately, last 60 days (extendable once to 120), and become law only if Congress approves. Sources: Library of Congress, Global Legal Monitor, Constitution of Brazil, Art. 62 (Constitute Project).

Real cases never match a game scenario exactly, so each debrief says how yours differs. If you spot an error, we want to hear about it.

Constitution Builder: real-world echoes

The builder shows two short echoes for each rule and a few more in its “Watch for” notes. Each one describes a single rule, never a whole country or a visitor’s whole design, and none of them is a verdict.

How echoes are sourced. Every echo has two kinds of link where they exist:

  • The rule: the actual constitutional clause, linked on the Constitute Project, a free, neutral, searchable collection of the world’s constitutions. Where Constitute doesn’t hold the text (Magna Carta, the UK Parliament Acts), we link the official text instead.
  • What happened: an established reference, such as Encyclopaedia Britannica, a government or parliamentary archive, an electoral authority, or an academic or research summary. For very recent events we use a major news report until a reference work covers them.

Wikipedia is a good place to start looking, but we don’t cite it. Some cases (ancient Athens, Rome, Venice, France in 1789) have no written constitutional clause to link, so they carry an event source only. The full method is in the sourcing policy.

What’s invented in the builder. The countries, people, parties and election results the builder generates are invented. Its seat generator is a simple model, not a forecast. The one-sentence echo texts are our own summaries of the sources below.
